CMR Convention (1956)

The CMR Convention (from French Convention relative au contrat de transport international de marchandises par route) is the Convention on the Contract for the International Carriage of Goods by Road, signed in Geneva on 19 May 1956. It is the principal international instrument governing the carriage of goods by road between contracting states: it establishes a uniform consignment note, the terms of the contract of carriage, and the carrier's liability. The Russian Federation is a party to the Convention (as the legal successor to the USSR, which ratified it in 1983).

Scope of application (art. 1)

The Convention applies to the carriage of goods by road for reward where two conditions are met simultaneously:

  • the place of taking over the goods and the place of delivery are situated in two different countries;
  • at least one of those countries is a contracting party to the CMR Convention.

The Convention applies regardless of the place of residence and nationality of the parties to the contract (sender, carrier, consignee). This means that if goods move, for example, from Russia (a contracting party) to a third country, the CMR applies even where the carrier is a company from a state that has not acceded to the Convention.

What falls OUTSIDE the CMR (art. 1, para. 4)

The Convention does not apply to:

  • carriage of postal consignments under international postal conventions;
  • carriage of the deceased;
  • carriage of furniture removals (household effects in the course of a move).

The CMR consignment note

The contract of carriage is confirmed by the CMR consignment note. Key rules:

  • Three copies (art. 5) — signed by the sender and the carrier: the first for the sender, the second accompanying the goods, the third for the carrier. In practice, additional copies are often made (for customs, settlement, the freight forwarder).
  • Particulars (art. 6) — the date and place of issue, the names and addresses of the sender, carrier and consignee, a description of the goods (nature, packing, number of packages), gross weight, places of loading and unloading, related charges, customs instructions, etc.
  • The absence or irregularity of the consignment note does not affect the validity of the contract of carriage itself (art. 4): the contract exists even without a note, and the CMR remains the evidential document.
Important: the CMR note is not a document of title (unlike a bill of lading). It cannot be "transferred" by endorsement as a right to the goods; it merely records the contract of carriage and the taking over of the goods.

Carrier's liability (art. 17–29)

Period of liability

The carrier is liable for the goods from the moment of taking them over until the moment of delivery to the consignee (art. 17, para. 1), and for delay in delivery.

Presumption of fault

The CMR operates a presumption of fault of the carrier: loss, damage or delay are presumed to have been caused by the carrier unless he proves otherwise. The burden of proof lies with the carrier.

Grounds for exoneration (art. 17, paras. 2, 4)

The carrier is relieved of liability if he proves that the loss or damage was caused by:

  • the fault of the person entitled, or an instruction of his (sender/consignee);
  • defective packing or containers;
  • the special inherent nature of the goods;
  • circumstances which the carrier could not avoid and the consequences of which he was unable to prevent (force majeure).

"Special risks" are singled out separately (art. 17, para. 4): carriage in open vehicles, absence or damage to packing, loading/stowage by the sender, the nature of perishable goods, etc. — for these there is a presumption of exoneration of the carrier where the goods were carried under the agreed conditions.

Limit of liability — 8.33 SDR per kilogram

For loss of or damage to goods, compensation is limited to 8.33 units of account (SDR) per kilogram of gross weight of the lost or damaged goods (art. 23, para. 3). Carriage charges, customs duties and other costs connected with the carriage are refunded in addition. For delay in delivery, compensation does not exceed the amount of the carriage charges (art. 23, para. 5).

SDR (Special Drawing Rights) — what they are and how they work

The SDR (Special Drawing Rights) is an international unit of account created by the International Monetary Fund (IMF) in 1969 as a supplement to the official reserves of member countries. It is not a currency and not a means of payment — the SDR has no banknotes or coins.

The currency basket

The value of the SDR is determined on the basis of a basket of five world currencies (the composition is reviewed by the IMF every five years):

  • the US dollar;
  • the euro;
  • the Chinese yuan (added to the basket in 2016);
  • the Japanese yen;
  • the pound sterling.

How the rate is determined

The IMF calculates and publishes the SDR exchange rate against the major currencies daily, based on market quotes of the basket currencies. To convert a liability limit into a national currency, one takes the current SDR rate (published by the IMF and central banks) and multiplies it by the number of kilograms.

Why SDRs are used in conventions

SDRs are used so that liability limits in international transport conventions are not eroded by the inflation of a single currency and do not depend on exchange-rate fluctuations. Besides the CMR, the SDR is used in the Montreal Convention (aviation) and the Hague-Visby Rules (sea carriage); within the CMR itself, the 1978 Protocol replaced the former unit (the Poincaré franc) with the SDR.

Example: goods weighing 1,000 kg gross are lost. The limit = 1,000 × 8.33 = 8,330 SDR. If 1 SDR ≈ USD 1.35, the maximum compensation ≈ USD 11,245 (the exact figure is calculated at the rate on the date of settlement).

Reservations on taking over and delivering the goods (art. 8–9, 30)

  • On taking over the goods the carrier checks the apparent condition of the goods and their packing, the number of packages and the marks (art. 8). Where a check is impossible, he enters a reasoned reservation in the note.
  • A note containing no reservations is prima facie evidence that the goods and their packing were in apparent good condition on taking over (art. 9).
  • Where the goods are received without reservations, they are deemed to have been delivered in accordance with the note (art. 30) — therefore damage must be recorded before signing the delivery documents.

Limitation periods (art. 32)

  • The general period is one year from the date of delivery of the goods (or the date on which they should have been delivered).
  • In the case of the carrier's wilful misconduct, or default considered equivalent to wilful misconduct, the period is three years.

Successive carriers (art. 34–40)

Where carriage is performed by several carriers successively under a single CMR note, each carrier is liable for the whole of the journey, and their liability is joint and several. A claim may be brought against the first carrier, the last carrier, or the carrier on whose leg the loss or damage occurred.

The 1978 Protocol

The Protocol to the CMR Convention (Geneva, 1978) replaced the former unit of account (the Poincaré franc) with the SDR. This is why all current limits in the CMR are expressed in SDRs.

Practical significance: for international road carriage between CMR states, a contract of carriage under the Convention and a CMR consignment note are mandatory.
