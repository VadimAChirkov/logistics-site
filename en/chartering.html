Ship chartering

Types of contract of carriage by sea

Under a contract of carriage of goods, the carrier undertakes to deliver the goods entrusted to it by the shipper to the port of destination and to hand them over to the person entitled to receive them (the consignee), and the shipper undertakes to pay the agreed freight for the carriage of the goods.

Under a contract of affreightment (charter party), one party (the carrier, the shipowner) undertakes, for payment, to place at the disposal of the other party (the charterer) all or part of the cargo capacity of one or more vessels for one or more voyages for the carriage of goods or baggage.

Either the shipowner or another legal entity having the right to manage the operation of the vessel for the whole term of the contract of carriage may act as the sea carrier. In the standard forms of voyage charter parties, the term "shipowner" (or "owner") is used in all such cases. The charterer may be the seller or buyer of the goods, a multimodal transport operator, a broker or a freight forwarder.

The conclusion of a contract of carriage by sea is evidenced by the issue to the shipper of a bill of lading, while a contract of affreightment is documented by a voyage charter party or a fixture recap with reference to a standard charter-party form.

Bill of Lading (B/L)

The bill of lading is used as a contract of carriage by sea, usually in liner shipping. The shipowner acts as a common carrier. It independently determines the composition of the fleet on the line, the ports of call, the frequency of voyages, the schedule, develops the commercial terms of the B/L and the tariffs, and offers the transport service system it has organised to all shippers. Individual consignments of cargo are stowed on board by the carrier (master) at its discretion, in the general interest of the voyage. Only the carriage of cargo on deck must be agreed with the cargo owner, and a corresponding note ("on deck") is made on the face of the bill of lading. After accepting a given consignment, the carrier is entitled to load or discharge any other cargo in this or another port of the line, to perform various auxiliary operations and repairs, and to deliver the cargo with transshipment at an intermediate port onto another vessel or another mode of transport. The terms of carriage and the tariffs apply to all shippers for a long period — until changed. In certain cases (a regular shipper, a large consignment, under-loading of the vessel, or the presence of empty containers in port) the line may agree significant discounts on the published tariff rates with the customer.

Voyage Charter Party (C/P)

The voyage charter party is used in liner and tramp shipping for the carriage of bulk cargo. The contract is concluded on the basis of the charterer's offer, which defines all the main conditions of the forthcoming voyage: the kind and quantity of cargo, the ports of loading and discharge, the date the cargo will be ready for carriage, the rates of cargo work, and the requirements for the vessel. In general, under a C/P the shipowner acts as a contract carrier. The charterer is provided with the whole vessel, or specific cargo spaces, or a specific part of the vessel's capacity for the stowage of cargo. The carrier has no right to use the chartered vessel (or spaces) for other, even incidental, carriage, unless this is agreed with the charterer. If such cargo has been loaded on board, the charterer may demand its discharge, and if the carriage has already taken place — compensation for its proven losses (due to delay in the delivery of its cargo, damage to the cargo, mixing of cargoes). The carrier must provide, by the agreed date, a vessel fully prepared for the forthcoming voyage and, after completion of loading, immediately send it to the port of discharge by the usual route and at the usual speed.

Unless the contract provides for liner terms of vessel handling, the charterer is responsible for providing a berth at the ports of loading and/or discharge and for performing cargo work within the time (or at the rates) provided for in the charter, and also pays the cost of the cargo work.

The charter sets out in detail the rights and obligations of the parties in the event of any possible deviations of the actual conditions of the voyage from those agreed in the charter, such as: the absence of the required quantity of cargo, the unreadiness of the vessel or its arrival at the port of loading earlier than the agreed date, demurrage of the vessel awaiting a berth, delay of the vessel or early completion of cargo work, and various force-majeure circumstances. All the terms of the contract, as well as the freight rate, are agreed in detail by the carrier and the charterer and are valid only for the contracting parties and only for the period of the given transaction.

Three types of ship chartering under a voyage charter party

  • Chartering for a single voyage (one voyage C/P). After the cargo is delivered to the consignee and all settlements for the voyage are completed, both parties are released from mutual obligations.
  • Chartering a vessel for several consecutive voyages (consecutive voyage C/P). Under this contract the operator must, after completion of the first voyage, immediately send the vessel on the second, then the third, and so on until the whole agreed number of voyages is performed. The freight is paid separately for each voyage at pre-agreed rates. Throughout the term of the contract, cargo of other owners may not be carried on the vessel without the charterer's agreement, even in the same or the reverse ballast direction. The substitution of the vessel stipulated in the contract by a substitute in the first or any subsequent voyage must also be agreed with the charterer.
  • Long-term or general freight contract (contract of affreightment) — see a real COA example in the section "Chartering operations technique". It is an agreement on the carriage, by regular voyages, of a specified quantity of a given charterer's cargo during an agreed calendar period; to perform its obligations the carrier is entitled to use not only its own but also chartered tonnage and to substitute vessels during the term of the contract. The main terms of a freight contract: the term of the contract with the specific dates of its beginning and end; the ports (ranges) of loading and discharge; the kind and total quantity of cargo and the distribution of carriage by month (in tonnes or by number of voyages); the type or deadweight of the vessels the carrier may use to perform the contract; and the minimum interval for the presentation of vessels for loading. The contract may set restrictions on the age and dimensions of the vessels (length, draught when fully laden), requirements for suitability for cargo operations, for the carriage of palletised cargo, for an ice class, etc.

The charterer enters these conditions into the contract: they guarantee that the shipowner will not present two or more vessels for loading at the same time, that their deadweight will correspond to the sizes of the cargo consignments, and that any vessel presented to it will meet the characteristics of the port, the sailing conditions and the cargo transshipment technology. In turn, the shipowner stipulates the maximum number of ports of call per voyage, the minimum estimated loading of the vessel for the carriage of the last consignment of cargo, and other conditions.

The contract is drawn up on the basis of one of the standard charter-party forms and defines uniform conditions of carriage for the whole term of the contract.

Short forms of contract of carriage by sea

The liner bill of lading and the voyage charter party are the full form of a contract of carriage by sea, since they contain all the necessary commercial and legal clauses defining the relationship of the parties to the given transaction. In addition, there are short forms of contract. In them only the main characteristics of the transaction are agreed, while the remaining conditions are governed by reference to a charter party or bill of lading. Such forms include:

  • Booking note — a preliminary application by the cargo owner to reserve space on a vessel for a specific consignment of cargo. It is used usually in liner shipping. After it is signed by the carrier or its agent, the booking note acquires the character of a contract of carriage by sea. It sets out the specific characteristics of the transaction: the quantity and kind of cargo, the ports of loading and discharge, the date the cargo is ready, the name of the vessel, and the freight rate. As to the remaining conditions of carriage, reference is made to the bill of lading of the line.
  • Berth note — a contract for the carriage of incidental bulk cargo. It is used in both liner and tramp shipping, usually for loading at the same port (at the same berth) where the main cargo is accepted. Unlike the booking note, it contains a number of clauses typical of charter carriage: the procedure for calculating laytime, the allocation of cargo-work costs, and the freight rate. As to the remaining commercial and legal conditions, the berth note refers to one of the standard charter-party forms.

All types of contract of carriage by sea define the rights, liability and risks of the shipowner (carrier).

Standard voyage charter-party forms

When concluding a voyage charter party, the shipowner and the charterer must agree in detail their mutual obligations as to the vessel, the cargo, the ports of loading and discharge, the procedure for calculating laytime and paying freight, the allocation of stevedoring costs, etc. For each of these items it is necessary to provide for the various situations that may arise in the forthcoming voyage and the liability of the parties for breach of obligations. The wording of the contract must guarantee its unambiguous interpretation and take into account the practice of arbitration and judicial bodies.

The limited time available to the parties for negotiations makes it practically impossible to formulate afresh all the conditions of the contract on concluding each specific transaction. Therefore the use of standard charter-party forms is generally accepted in modern merchant shipping. These are standard charter conditions developed on the basis of long international experience, which the partners may use as a starting basis for negotiations when concluding a transaction. The standard forms are developed by international organisations of shipowners and brokers or by large charterers with the involvement of specialists in maritime law, which ensures a high professional level of these documents. Their use does not deprive the parties of the possibility of making individual amendments to the charter, entered in the text or included as special additions. The introduction of amendments or additions to the charter automatically nullifies all conditions contradicting them that are contained in the printed text of the charter, even if this was not specifically stipulated.

As a rule, the clauses of the charter and the lines of the printed text are numbered, which simplifies the agreement of the necessary changes to the printed text (see a real COA example in the section "Chartering operations technique"), especially when negotiations are conducted by means of communication (e-mail, telephone). The main task of the charterer and the shipowner in this case is to resolve the questions determining the specific content of the contract: the vessel, the time of its presentation for loading, the rates of cargo work, the freight rate and the demurrage rate. As to the commercial and legal conditions of carriage, it is sufficient for the parties to choose a standard charter-party form and agree the changes and additions to be made to its printed text.

Structure of a charter party

Modern charter parties consist of three sections: the preamble, which states the parties and the place and date of conclusion of the contract; part one, which contains all the variable conditions characterising the content of the given specific transaction (the vessel, the cargo, the date the vessel is ready, the calculation of laytime, the payment of freight); and part two, which contains the procedure for applying the commercial conditions of the transaction and the standard clauses of a legal nature: on general average, on mutual liability in the event of collision of vessels, etc. Usually, on concluding a charter party, the legal clauses are accepted by the partners in full according to the printed text of the given charter-party form, or with minimal changes.

The modern form of a charter party facilitates its use in operational conditions, since all information on the content of the transaction is brought together on a single first page. In addition, the preparation of copies of the charter is facilitated, since the whole constant part may be reproduced in advance by printing, while on concluding each specific contract it is sufficient to complete only the single first page, in which the variable conditions are set out. In recent years a special form of charter party has been used, where special boxes (the so-called box form) are provided on the first page for recording each kind of variable information. This kind of layout is the most convenient from the point of view of the clarity of the material, and also for completing and processing charter parties.

Addendum and rider

When agreeing the commercial conditions of a charter party, especially if the negotiations are conducted on the basis of an outdated form, it becomes necessary to change a large number of standard formulations. Making numerous additions and corrections to the printed text complicates the completion and study of the charter. Therefore the accepted practice is that the relevant conditions of the standard form are simply struck out and new ones are formulated instead. All newly formulated or additional clauses of the contract are combined into a single supplement to the charter party (an addendum or rider). The clauses included in the addendum are numbered consecutively, continuing the numbering of the clauses of the standard form. On the last page of the standard form (in the box form — in a special box on the first page of the standard charter) the numbers of the clauses of the addendum are given and it is stated that they are an integral part of the charter party.

Most often the addendum formulates the following conditions of the contract: a full description of the vessel; the terms of payment of freight; the allocation of cargo-work costs; the calculation of laytime; the inspection of the readiness of the holds by the charterer's surveyor; the allocation of the costs of dues and charges on the cargo, freight and vessel; the payment of extra insurance; the relationship of the parties in the event of damage to the vessel by the charterer's stevedores, etc.

Riders are usually used to formalise private additions to the charter party for a given specific voyage, as a rule confidential, for example the terms of payment of freight.

Commercial conditions of charter parties

1. Seaworthiness and characteristics of vessels

The carrier must, in good time before the voyage, put the vessel in a seaworthy condition: ensure the technical fitness of the vessel for the voyage, properly equip the vessel, man it with a crew and supply it with everything necessary, and also bring the holds and other spaces of the vessel in which the cargo is carried into a condition ensuring the proper receipt, carriage and preservation of the cargo.

The carrier is not liable for the unseaworthiness of the vessel if it proves that the unseaworthiness was caused by defects that could not be discovered by the exercise of due diligence (latent defects).

2. The concept of "seaworthiness" is composite, consisting of the following elements

  1. Preparation of the vessel as to hull and machinery — ensuring the stability, strength and watertightness of the hull, the soundness of the machinery, mechanisms, fuel, ballast and other systems of the vessel, the supply of the necessary provisions, water, fuel and equipment (apparatus, tools, inventory, replacement and spare parts, instructions, etc.), i.e. the requirements for the elements of the vessel that ensure its technical fitness for the voyage and safe navigation. Confirmation of the seaworthiness of the vessel is the valid documents of the classification society for the equipment and outfit, reports on repairs carried out, etc.
  2. Preparation of the cargo spaces, cargo gear and hatch covers for the carriage of the planned cargo. In other words, the possibility of carrying the given cargo in the given vessel. The readiness of the vessel to receive cargo is confirmed by a surveyor's certificate, and the readiness of the cargo gear by valid certificates.
  3. Manning of the vessel with a qualified crew, preparation of the crew and of the conditions for the crew.
  4. Compliance with the requirements of international organisations and national authorities. Here it is also necessary to take into account such characteristics as the flag, class and age of the vessel. Thus, the impossibility of the vessel's entry into a port because of a boycott of the vessel's flag, or a prohibition on the entry into port of vessels without an ice class, may be regarded as a case of unseaworthiness, i.e. unreadiness for the given voyage.

The existing forms for the carriage of cargo or the hire of a vessel solve the problem of formulating the provisions on the seaworthiness of the vessel by entering the necessary requirements for the vessel into the form. A tendency has been noted: the newer the form, the more clauses in it are devoted to the requirements for the seaworthiness of the vessel. Thus, questions of seaworthiness are regulated in parallel by international conventions, regulatory legal acts and customs, on the one hand, and by the contract, on the other.

3. Date the vessel is ready to load

In a charter party, the time the vessel is ready to load is set by the shipowner not as a specific date but as an interval between two calendar dates. The first date is briefly called laydays (the full text in the charter-party form is "laydays no commenced before…"). If the vessel arrives at the port earlier, the charterer has the right not to accept it, and laytime will begin to run only after the laydays date. The second date is called the cancelling date (i.e. the date for termination of the contract). If the vessel is not ready to load by that date, the charterer has the right to cancel the charter party.

Usually the charter states that laydays begin at the start of the official working hours of the port (e.g. from 8 a.m.) and that the cancelling date expires at the end of the official working hours of the port (e.g. at 4 p.m.). In the course of the negotiations on concluding the charter, the whole interval is called laycan (l/c) and is set as a single whole (e.g. 10–25.06.2026).

To avoid disputes, the charter should clearly stipulate the procedure for changing the laycan dates. If the vessel arrived at the port before the laydays date and was placed under loading by the charterer, the contract may provide for one of three options for the calculation of laytime:

  • a condition in the shipowner's interest: all time actually used for loading before the laydays date is included in laytime;
  • a condition in the charterer's interest: the time before the laydays date is not counted, regardless of whether it was used or not;
  • a compromise: half of the time used before the start of laydays is included in laytime.

If necessary, the charterer should include in the addendum to the charter the condition: "no NOR to be tendered before laydays".

4. Cargo

As a rule, the contract is concluded for the carriage of a specific cargo. The shipowner is interested in the charter stating not only the full and precise name of the cargo but also all its transport characteristics, i.e. the physical and chemical properties of the cargo that determine the possible loading of the vessel, the duration and cost of the cargo work, and the risks associated with the carriage of that cargo for the given cargo, vessel, crew or other cargoes. First of all the method of carriage and the kind of packing are stated: in bulk, in separate packages, in pallets, big bags or containers. When carrying measurement cargo (stowage factor (SF) over 1.3 m³/t) the charter must state the estimated SF; for round timber — its length and diameter and whether the logs are debarked or not; for sawn timber in packages — the type of package (squared or the so-called tractor package), with dunnage between each row of boards (on stickers) or not, and the size and mass of the package; for metal and other general cargo the type (packages, coils, bundles, bales, bags), dimensions and mass of one piece are stated; for bulk cargo — the SF, the permissible angle of repose, the percentage of moisture; for heavy lifts and long lengths — the method of carriage (crated or not), the dimensions and mass of each piece, and the admissibility of carriage on deck. Special attention must be paid to a full description of the cargo when carrying dangerous goods.

Sometimes the charter stipulates that the shipper has the right to present any lawful cargo for carriage. This means that the cargo must pass customs (and, if necessary, quarantine) control both at the port of loading and at the port of destination.

As a rule, the charter stipulates a specific quantity of cargo — in metric tonnes, cubic metres (timber cargoes) or in units (containers, vehicles). For general cargo the precise mass is stated, or the qualification "about" is used. In that case a deviation from the stated quantity of ±1–3% is implied, depending on the kind of cargo.

Bulk cargoes (dry bulk, timber, liquid, scrap metal) are sold and bought under large, long-term contracts. Therefore the charter usually stipulates a margin — the permissible deviation from the basic quantity of cargo agreed between the shipowner and the charterer for the given voyage. The margin is ±5–10%.

Sometimes the charter stipulates that the quantity of cargo within the margin is chosen by the charterer (the condition MOLCHOPT — more or less charterer's option).

If the shipowner cannot determine in advance how much of a given cargo it will stow on the vessel (unnamed cargo, equipment, etc.), it enters into the charter the condition "full and complete cargo (f+c)". This means that it undertakes to accept as much cargo as is permissible by the deadweight or cargo capacity of the vessel.

The stowage of part of the cargo on deck is permitted only with the charterer's consent. A corresponding reservation must be entered in the charter (sometimes stating the percentage of the total quantity of cargo). In the bill of lading a stamp "On deck" is made.

If the charterer has not presented the whole quantity of cargo declared by the master within the margin, the shipowner is entitled to dead freight. It is calculated as freight at the rate agreed in the charter for the whole quantity of cargo not loaded, less the expenses that the shipowner would have incurred in such carriage.

5. Ports of loading and discharge

The ports of loading and discharge may be set in the charter in one of four ways:

  • Direct ports — specific ports.
  • Option of the port — the charter lists several possible ports of loading/discharge, and during the voyage the charterer has the right to send the vessel to any of them. For example, Hamburg or Bremen; Antwerp, Rotterdam, Amsterdam (ARA).
  • Range — the charter states a section of the sea coast bounded by two ports, or a sea basin. During the voyage the charterer has the right to send the vessel to any port of that section, including both bounding ports. For example, the Hamburg–Le Havre range.
  • Broad c/p (wide charter) — an option of ranges. The charter states several ranges, and during the voyages the charterer may send the vessel to any port of any of those ranges.

If the possible ports of loading/discharge are not specifically named in the charter — a range or a wide charter — the shipowner must clearly stipulate the requirements for the port. At present it is generally accepted in such cases to include in the charter the clause "good, safe, always afloat, always accessible port" (gsaaaap — good, safe, always afloat, always accessible port). This means that the port nominated by the charterer must have transshipment equipment; be safe in the military, navigational and quarantine senses; be accessible in any weather and at any time of day; and the depths at the berth must permit cargo operations always afloat.

6. Laytime

Laytime is the time which, in accordance with the terms of the charter, is allowed to the charterer for the performance of stevedoring operations at the ports of loading and discharge, for which the shipowner receives no additional remuneration other than the agreed freight rate.

Preliminary notices. In order for the running of laytime to begin in accordance with the charter, the master must give, at the port of loading (discharge), to the addresses stated in the charter, preliminary notices of the expected date of arrival of the vessel at the port. The number and timing of these notices are stipulated in the contract. Most often the giving of 2–3 notices is provided for, e.g. 15, 10 and 5 days in advance. In that case the last of them is called the final notice (or definite notice). When the voyage is short, the giving of only one preliminary notice may be agreed — after leaving the last previous port.

The charter specifically stipulates the master's obligation to declare the expected time of arrival of the vessel at the port (ETA). This notice must be given 24 and/or 48 hours before the vessel's approach and, as a rule, is not subject to correction. If the vessel is late against the declared time, the charterer is often entitled to demand payment of proven losses for the idle time of gangs, lighters, wagons and the storage of cargo.

Notice of readiness (NOR). The second important condition for the beginning of laytime is the giving by the master of a notice of readiness of the vessel for cargo operations, which must be accepted by the charterer. Depending on the agreement of the parties and the customs of the port, the charter may provide for the following alternative conditions as to the place of giving the notice of readiness: after obtaining free pratique and mooring the vessel to the berth; regardless of whether the vessel is at the berth or not, i.e. from an anchorage in the port waters (the WIBON condition); regardless of whether the vessel is in the port or not, i.e. from an open roadstead within sight of the pilot station (the WIPON condition); whether it has obtained free pratique or not (WIFPON); whether it has passed customs control or not (WICCON).

In recent years shipowners have most often entered the conditions WIPON, WIBON, WIFPON, WICCON (abbreviated WWWW) into charters or addenda. They give the master the right to tender notice of readiness of the vessel for cargo operations even if the vessel is at an inner or outer roadstead and has not passed sanitary and customs control.

Period of grace. Under the conditions of the standard charter-party forms, the charterer is allowed a certain period of grace after the acceptance of the notice of readiness and before the commencement of laytime, which is connected with the practice of ports in booking work gangs in advance. Most often the period of grace is formulated as follows: "if the notice of readiness is accepted before noon, laytime begins to run from 1 (2) p.m.; if the notice is accepted after noon, laytime begins to run from 6 (8) a.m. on the following working day". However, in some specialised charter-party forms other formulations are used, e.g. from 8 a.m. on the following working day, or 24 working hours after the acceptance of the notice.

The printed text of the Gencon form states that all time spent waiting for a berth is counted as loading/discharging time. It is in the charterer's interest to change this formulation to "laytime…" (further in the text). In that case all stoppages excluded from laytime (bad weather, weekends) will not be counted during the vessel's stay at the roadstead either.

Calculation of laytime. The calculation of the duration of laytime may be stipulated in the charter at a rate per hatch per day (hour); at a rate per vessel per day (hour); as a number of days (hours); according to the customs of the port; or at customary speed.

Usually only working weather days (WWD — weather working day) are counted as laytime, i.e. the charterer is released from liability for the idle time of the vessel on weekends, holidays and days of bad weather. Recently a more precise formulation is used: "weather permitting".

Where the charter provides for the loading or discharging of the vessel at several ports, laytime is set as a whole for the relevant kind of cargo operation, and the running of laytime at the second and subsequent ports of loading (discharge) usually begins immediately after the acceptance of the notice of readiness, without a period of grace. Sometimes laytime is calculated as a whole for the entire voyage. This method of calculation is called "reversible".

Excluded periods. Days of bad weather, as well as Sundays and holidays, are excluded from laytime (Sundays, holidays excepted — SHEX). If, under the contract, Sundays and holidays are included in laytime, the clause SHINC (Sundays, holidays included) is entered in the charter. If the running of laytime began during the vessel's stay at the roadstead, the time of the passage from the anchorage to the berth, as well as the time of customs and sanitary formalities and the time of acceptance of the holds by the charterer's surveyors, etc., are excluded from laytime.

If the charter provides for the charterer's right to load (discharge) the vessel at several berths, the addendum to the charter should state whether the time of shifting berths is included in laytime or not. In all cases any delays or interruptions in cargo work caused by the vessel are excluded from laytime: due to a defect of the vessel's cargo gear or hatch covers, or the absence of electric power. Also charged to the shipowner is the time for shifting along the berth, lightering, and trimming of cargo, if this is caused by the non-conformity of the vessel's characteristics with the terms of the charter (as to length, draught, suitability for grab discharge). Interruptions in cargo operations caused by the charterer, such as the unreadiness or absence of cargo, the absence of labour or vehicles, etc., are not excluded from laytime.

When laytime is calculated in working days, the charterer may, if necessary, perform cargo work also on Sundays and holidays. In these cases the question arises whether the time used for work should be included in laytime. It may be stipulated that Sundays and holidays are excluded from laytime regardless of whether they were used for work or not (whether used or not). The other, opposite approach is expressed in the condition that Sundays and holidays are excluded from laytime only if they were not used for work (unless used). In that case, if the charterer performed work on Sundays and holidays, all time actually used for work is included in laytime. Usually, to remove possible disputes, such a clause is entered in the charter in expanded form: "Unless used, if used, all time actually used shall count".

The detailed accounting of the vessel's time in port from the moment of mooring to the berth (dropping anchor) until the end of cargo operations and the completion of the cargo documents is recorded in a special document — the statement of facts. On its basis a time sheet is drawn up, which, in addition to information on the actual duration of the vessel's stay, gives the calculation of laytime and the amount of despatch (demurrage).

Regardless of the form of this document, which differs among shipowners, the time sheet must contain the following data:

  • the time, i.e. the date, hours and minutes of the vessel's mooring to the berth or anchoring at the roadstead (in the latter case the time of the vessel's mooring to the berth is stated separately);
  • the time of the master's tendering the notice of readiness and of its acceptance by the consignor's (consignee's) agent;
  • the charter rate of cargo work, the total quantity of cargo and the duration of laytime;
  • the time of the actual beginning and end and the total duration of the cargo work;
  • daily data on the duration of laytime and of the periods excluded from laytime;
  • the calculation of the amount of demurrage or despatch on the results of the handling of the vessel.

The time sheet is signed by the shipowner (its broker) and sent to the charterer for agreement and payment.

7. Payment of despatch/demurrage

If the vessel is delayed under loading or discharging beyond the agreed laytime, the so-called time on demurrage begins, for which the charterer pays the shipowner demurrage, which is an additional payment to the agreed freight for the delay of the vessel. The amount of demurrage is stated in the charter as a specific sum per vessel per day, or less often as a rate per 1 gross registered tonne of capacity, and is paid for each day of idle time and proportionally for part of a day. In a number of charters for the carriage of cargo with high rates of cargo work (ore, coal), an hourly rather than a daily demurrage rate is set. Time on demurrage is always counted in running days, so from the moment laytime expires demurrage is payable for all days without exception — Sundays, holidays, days of bad weather, etc. It is understood that if the vessel had been handled on time it would not have been in port during those weekends or days of bad weather. The only thing excluded from time on demurrage is stoppages caused by the vessel.

Some charter-party forms stipulate a certain maximum number of days on demurrage, i.e. days during which the charterer has the right to detain the vessel against payment of demurrage, e.g. under the Gencon-76 charter — 10 running days at the ports of loading and discharge together. On the expiry of the stipulated number of days on demurrage the shipowner is entitled, having warned the charterer, to send the vessel out of the port of loading with the quantity of cargo already loaded and to demand payment of dead freight for the short loading. However, the parties usually agree on the amount of compensation payable by the charterer for the detention of the vessel beyond the time on demurrage. Such detention is called detention beyond the time on demurrage (detention), and for each day of such delay payment is provided at a rate exceeding the demurrage rate by a certain percentage, or in the form of a specific additional daily rate paid on top of the demurrage rate. The charterer is interested in removing from the printed text of the charter-party form the indication of the maximum number of days on demurrage.

Gencon-94 provides that demurrage is paid immediately upon receipt of the shipowner's invoice. Otherwise the shipowner must give the charterer written notice, and on the expiry of 96 hours, if the vessel is at the port of loading, it has the right to cancel the transaction and present all its losses to the charterer.

Despatch money is remuneration paid by the shipowner to the charterer for completing the loading or discharging of the vessel before the expiry of the laytime stipulated by the charter, i.e. for the so-called "saved time". The amount of despatch is usually set at half the demurrage rate, on the consideration that the shipowner should share with the charterer the additional benefit obtained as a result of the early handling of the vessel. The basis for receiving despatch must be the existence of a corresponding condition in the charter.

Shipowners have recently been seeking a clause in the charter abolishing despatch (free despatch), or at least reducing its amount. Instead of the previously common condition that despatch is paid for all time saved (all time saved), charters now stipulate its payment only for working time saved (working time saved), i.e. excluding non-working days.

8. Payment of cargo work

The variants for allocating the costs of loading and discharging cargo between the shipowner and the charterer used in voyage charter parties may be reduced to the following six standard schemes:

  • the shipowner bears the costs of cargo work both at the port of loading and at the port of discharge (gross terms);
  • the shipowner is free of the cost of loading, but bears the costs of stowing the cargo and of discharging it (free in);
  • the shipowner is free of the cost of loading and stowing the cargo, but bears the costs of discharging (free in and stowed);
  • the shipowner is free of the cost of discharging, but pays for loading and stowing the cargo (free out);
  • the shipowner is free of the costs of loading and discharging the cargo, but pays for its stowage — free in and out (fio);
  • the shipowner is free of the cost of loading, discharging and stowing the cargo — free in and out and stowed (fios) — most frequently used.

The charter specifically stipulates the allocation of the costs of separation, dunnage and securing (unsecuring) materials. In most standard charter-party forms these costs are charged to the charterer (FIOS L/S/D).

In performing cargo operations it is often necessary to resort to overtime. The shipowner may need this to avoid, for example, the vessel's being delayed in port over Saturday and Sunday or other holidays, in connection with a long wait for a spring tide to leave the port, or the threat of the vessel's being late for the cancelling date of the next voyage, etc. The charterer may resort to overtime in order to keep within the allowed laytime and to ship the goods within the time provided for by the sale contract. The usual condition contained in charters is the payment of overtime by the party ordering it (overtime for account of the party ordering same). Such practice occurs even in the absence of a corresponding condition in the charter.

9. Freight

In voyage chartering, the freight is set by agreement of the parties most often as a rate of freight per unit of cargo. The freight units are usually units of mass, volume or a piece of cargo. In liner shipping, for the carriage of especially valuable cargoes, freight is also calculated as a percentage of the value of the cargo (ad valorem).

Since, for a number of reasons, the quantity of cargo received at the port of destination does not always exactly correspond to the quantity of cargo delivered at the port of loading, the charter stipulates for what quantity of cargo the freight is paid: for the loaded (intaken) quantity, i.e. the bill-of-lading quantity of cargo, or for the delivered quantity at the port of destination.

Where it is impossible to establish in advance how much cargo the vessel can take, or it is clear in advance that neither the deadweight nor the cargo capacity of the vessel can be properly used, the freight is stipulated as a total sum for the voyage, which is called "lumpsum".

The time of payment of freight may be stipulated differently in the charter. The shipowner is interested in receiving payment of freight at the port of loading immediately after the signing of the bills of lading, or within a stipulated number of days after the signing of the bills of lading for the shipped cargo. Usually the formula "freight payable w/i 3 (5) days after signing B/L but in any case BBB (before breaking bulk)" is used, i.e. in any case before the start of discharge. This clause gives the shipowner the right not to permit discharge until the freight is paid, with all the vessel's idle time for that reason being included in laytime. The addendum to the charter must stipulate that the B/L should bear the note "Freight payable as per charterparty".

The charterer's obligation to pay freight cannot be linked to its right to receive compensation for damaged cargo or other losses caused to it. Freight is paid in full even if the charterer has justified claims against the shipowner. The shipowner may insure its risks of loss of freight in the event of the loss of all or part of the cargo, but such insurance relates only to the case of loss of the cargo in the course of carriage and does not protect against dishonest or insolvent charterers.

Where freight is paid at the port of loading, shipowners usually enter the following condition into the charter (addendum): "Freight is deemed earned upon the loading of the cargo and is not subject to deductions and is not returnable, whether the vessel and/or cargo are lost or not lost" (in correspondence the abbreviation DEDANRVAOLONL — deemed earned, discountless and non-returnable, vessel and/or cargo lost or not lost).

In addition to the payment of freight for the cargo, the charter provides for the payment by the charterer to the shipowner of the so-called dead freight, if it has not provided the vessel with the full cargo stipulated by the charter, taking into account the margin.

If the charterer has not provided the whole quantity of cargo provided for by the charter, then in order to obtain dead freight the agent and/or the master of the vessel must take the following measures:

  • Obtain a written declaration from the charterer that no more cargo will be delivered to the vessel.
  • After the end of loading, call in an independent surveyor and obtain from it an official report on the extent of the unused deadweight and cargo capacity of the vessel.
  • Enter into the bill of lading a reservation on the shipowner's claim for dead freight in connection with the short loading of the vessel. Such a reservation in the bill of lading enables the shipowner to exercise its lien on the cargo if the charterer fails to pay the dead freight in time and in full.
  • Enter into the charter a clause that dead freight and/or demurrage at the port of loading are paid by the charterer within a stipulated number of days after the signing of the bill of lading, but in any case before the opening of the holds at the port of discharge (the BBB condition — before breaking bulk).

10. Other conditions of the charter

Agents servicing the vessel at the ports of loading and discharge. The shipowner is interested in the vessel's being serviced by an agency company with which it has a corresponding long-term contract. But the usual practice is that the port agents are appointed by the charterer. The forwarders of the consignor and consignee can competently recommend a reliable agent, respectively at the ports of loading and discharge, or take upon themselves the agency of the vessel.

Large consignors usually insist that their agency companies be appointed under the charter to service the vessel in the ports. As a result of negotiations any possible variant may be entered into the charter by agreement of the parties:

  • the shipowner's agent at the ports of loading and discharge (owners agent both ends — abbreviated oabends or oabe);
  • the charterer's agent at both ports (charterers agent both ends — abbreviated chabends or chabe);
  • the shipowner's agent at one of the ports (loading or discharge), the charterer's agent at the other.

Taxes and charges. A standard condition is entered into the charter (addendum) that all charges and dues on the vessel are paid by the shipowner, and all charges and dues on the cargo and freight are paid by the charterer.

Sometimes the charterer stipulates that the dues on freight are paid by the shipowner. In that case the shipowner may demand a corresponding increase in the freight rate. Under the Gencon-94 form, box 23 of part 1 of the charter must state whether the tax on freight is levied on the shipowner.

Extra insurance of cargo. The question of the extra insurance of cargo for the age, class and/or flag of the vessel is specifically stipulated. The addendum states at whose expense it is paid: the shipowner's, the charterer's, or at the shipowner's expense but not more than a stipulated sum (a compromise). Also, as a compromise, the clause "extra insurance charterer's account but owners to contribute USD…" may be entered into the addendum to the charter.

Brokerage commission. On signing the charter, the broker through whom the transaction was concluded is stated in it. The brokerage commission is set as a specific percentage charged on all payments due to the shipowner — freight, dead freight and demurrage. Often two brokers take part in concluding a transaction — one on the shipowner's side and one on the charterer's side. The charter may stipulate that the brokerage commission is paid directly by the charterer, which transfers to the shipowner only the remaining part of the freight (less commission).

Shipowners' liability. Shipowners are liable for the loss of or damage to cargo, or for delay in the delivery of cargo, only if such damage or delay was caused by a lack of due diligence on their part to make the vessel in all respects seaworthy, properly equipped, manned and supplied before the voyage, or if the damage and delay were caused by the personal acts or omissions of the shipowners or their manager in ensuring the preservation of the cargo.

Deviation. Unless otherwise agreed by the partners, the vessel has the right to call at ports in any order, to sail without pilots, to tow or render other assistance to vessels, and to deviate from the route for the purpose of saving life or property. Sometimes, in the course of negotiations, after the words "saving life" the shipowner adds "including the life of crew members", meaning that such deviation should not be regarded as a breach of the condition of the contract on the seaworthiness of the vessel before the voyage. In addition, after the word "property" the shipowner may write in "and also for bunkering", especially if at the port of loading there is a shortage of the necessary grades of fuel or high fuel prices are quoted.

Lien. The shipowner has a lien on the cargo as security for the payment of freight, dead freight, demurrage and claims for damage to the vessel, including all expenses for obtaining those payments. To exercise the lien the shipowner may either withhold the discharge of all or part of the cargo, or deliver such cargo at the port of discharge to the warehouses of the agent, the stevedoring company or the port with an instruction not to release the cargo to the consignee until the charterer has paid the whole amount of its debt.

Bills of lading. Bills of lading are issued on the basis of the standard form provided for by the given charter-party form. They are signed by the master of the vessel or by the shipowner's agent. In the latter case the shipowner must issue a written power of attorney to the agent, a copy of which the agent must present to the charterer. The charterers indemnify the shipowner for any losses that may arise because, under the bill of lading, more onerous obligations than those assumed by it under the charter may be imposed on the shipowner.

Mutual liability in collision. The clause establishes the principle of the carriers' liability to the cargo owners for damage to and loss of cargo on the colliding vessels in the amount of the established fault.

General average and the Jason clause. A standard clause making reference to the York-Antwerp Rules. Charters, as a rule, refer to the latest revision of the rules.

Often, in addition to the reference to the York-Antwerp Rules, this clause also states the place where the adjustment is to be made, e.g. London. Besides London, specialists (average adjusters) who can conduct general average adjustments are available in Moscow, New York and other places.

In the absence of an agreement of the parties, the relations arising from general average are governed by the law of the state in whose port the vessel ended its voyage after the occurrence giving rise to the general average.

When the vessel operates to US ports, the Jason clause must be included. Later it became known as the Jason clause. Subsequent decisions required certain changes to the text of the clause. It is now usually called the "new Jason clause".

General strike clause. As to a strike or lockout that may affect the duration of the voyage, three cases are considered. If a strike at the port of loading began at the time the vessel is ready to leave the previous port, or during the passage to the port of loading, or on the vessel's arrival at that port, the shipowners request the charterers' consent to count laytime as if there had been no strike (lockout). If the charterers do not give their consent in writing or by telegraph within 24 hours, the shipowners have the right to cancel the charter (i.e., if the charterer consents, the first 24 hours of the vessel's idle time are for the shipowner's account, the rest for the charterer's account). If part of the cargo has been loaded, the shipowners have the right to send the vessel on the voyage with that cargo (freight is paid only for the quantity actually loaded) and to complete the loading with incidental cargo in their own interest.

If a strike or lockout takes place at the port of discharge and is not settled within 48 hours, the charterers have the right to hold the vessel awaiting the end of the strike (lockout), paying half demurrage on the expiry of the time allowed for discharge until the end of the strike (lockout). After that, for the whole time until the complete end of discharge, demurrage is paid at the full rate. But the charterer has the right to send the vessel to another, safe port where it can discharge without the risk of delay because of the strike (lockout). Such instruction must be given to the vessel within 48 hours after the master or the shipowners notified the charterers that the strike is preventing discharge. If the cargo is delivered at the alternative port, all the conditions of the charter and bill of lading remain in force. However, if the sea passage to the new port exceeds 100 miles, the freight is increased in proportion to the increase in the total length of the voyage. Apart from the above obligations, neither the charterer nor the shipowner bears liability for any other consequences of the strike (lockout).

War risks (Voywar 1993). War risks include war or the threat of war, hostilities, civil war, acts of war, revolution, mutiny, civil unrest, the laying of mines, piracy, acts of terror, hostile actions and blockade — against all vessels or vessels of a specific flag, specific cargoes or crews, imposed by any person, body, terrorist or political group or government, which in the reasonable judgement of the master and/or the shipowner may be dangerous or may become dangerous to the vessel, its cargo, crew or other persons on board.

If war risk in a specific port or group of ports becomes known before the vessel begins loading, the shipowner may demand that the charterer replace those ports with other safe ones within the same range. If the charterer does not nominate such safe port or ports within 48 hours of the shipowner's notice, the latter has the right to cancel the contract or that part of it connected with the war risks.

If war risks become known after the start of loading, or during the passage to the port of discharge, or in the course of discharge, the shipowners are not obliged to continue loading (discharging), to sign bills of lading for an unsafe port, or to instruct the master to remain in an unsafe port or to continue to a place where the vessel, its cargo, crew or other persons on board will be exposed to war risks. The shipowners have the right to tender notice to the charterers demanding the nomination of a safe port for the discharge of the cargo or part of the cargo. If within 48 hours after the receipt of such notice the charterers do not nominate a safe port, the shipowners may discharge the accepted cargo or part of the cargo in any safe port of their choice (including the port of loading) with all the other conditions of the charter remaining in force. If the discharge is made at any port other than the port of loading, the shipowner is entitled to the full amount of freight. All additional expenses incurred in discharging the cargo at the port of loading or in delivering or discharging the cargo at any port must be paid by the charterer or the cargo owner. The shipowner has a lien on the cargo as security for all payments due to it. The shipowner also has the right to reimbursement of its additional expenses of discharge. As security for its additional expenses and freight, the shipowner may use the lien on the cargo.

If war risks in a specific region require the taking of a new, unusual route for the vessel, the shipowners must notify the charterer that such route will be taken. If the length of the voyage increases by more than 100 miles, the shipowners have the right to a proportional increase in the amount of freight.

On the occurrence of war risks, the vessel is entitled to comply with any orders and recommendations of the authorities, of war-risk insurers, of the UN Security Council, the European Union, or any body or group empowered to compel compliance with their orders or directions.

The shipowner has the right to discharge in any port cargo that may lead to the confiscation of the vessel as a carrier of contraband goods. The vessel may also call at any port to change the crew or part of it, or to put ashore other persons on board, if there are grounds to suppose that those persons may be interned, arrested or subjected to other sanctions.

General ice clause. If the port of loading has become inaccessible because of ice conditions at the time the vessel is ready to leave the last previous port, during the sea passage or on arrival at that port, the shipowner, because of the danger of being frozen in the ice, has the right to cancel the charter. If the danger of the vessel's being frozen in arose in the course of loading, the master has the right to take the vessel out of the port with the quantity of cargo on board and to complete the loading at any port in the shipowner's interest. The cargo loaded under the charter must be delivered to its destination without additional expense to the charterer, against payment of freight for the whole quantity of cargo delivered. If loading under the charter is to be made at two or more ports, some of which are closed by ice, the shipowner has the right to load only that part of the cargo that comes from the open port and then to complete the loading in its own interest at other ports, or to cancel the charter if the charterer does not agree to load the full cargo at the open port.

If ice prevents the vessel from reaching the port of discharge, the charterers have the right either to detain the vessel until navigation resumes, paying demurrage, or to send the vessel to another, safe and immediately accessible port where it can discharge without the risk of being detained by ice. The corresponding instruction must be given within 48 hours after the shipowners notified the charterers of the impossibility of reaching the original port of destination. If, in the course of discharge, because of the danger of being frozen in, the master considers it necessary to leave the port, it has the right to do so and to discharge the remaining cargo at the nearest safe port. After the delivery of the cargo at such port, all the conditions of the bill of lading remain in force, and the vessel must receive all the freight due. If the distance to the safe port exceeds 100 miles, the freight for the cargo delivered to that port is increased proportionally.

The ice clause is provided for cases where ice conditions at the agreed ports of loading and discharge are an unusual force-majeure circumstance. If the ice conditions in the given region and at the given time of year are the usual conditions of navigation, the ice clause is replaced by the icebreaker clause. This clause defines the vessel's obligation to follow an icebreaker in ice, establishes whether the vessel may tender notice of readiness from the ice edge, whether waiting for an icebreaker is included in laytime, who bears the risks when the vessel follows an icebreaker, etc.

Law and arbitration. This clause determines under the law of which country and in which city disputes between the partners under the charter are to be considered. The Gencon-94 charter provides for three variants. The dispute may be considered in London under English law, or in New York under American law, or in any other place by agreement of the parties. The chosen variant is stated on the face of the charter in the corresponding box. Under English law it is provided that if the parties have not agreed to appoint a single arbitrator, each party appoints one arbitrator and both of them appoint a third. The arbitration tribunal thus constituted reaches a decision unanimously or by a majority, and such decision is final. On receipt by one party of written notice of the appointment of an arbitrator by the other party, it must state its arbitrator within 14 days, otherwise the decision taken by the single arbitrator is final. Under American law the appointment of three arbitrators is mandatory, and a court order may be obtained to enforce the arbitrators' decision.
