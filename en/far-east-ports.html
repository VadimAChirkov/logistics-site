The Pacific scissors

While imports through Russia's Far East ports break records, exports are shrinking and the rate for a 40-foot container from Shanghai has nearly tripled in a year. We break down what is happening on the busiest route of Russian logistics — and why it matters for everyone shipping goods from Asia.

ANALYTICS · NOVEMBER 19, 2026 · Vadim Chirkov

125 thousand against 40 thousand

The dry numbers here speak louder than any forecast. In January–September 2026, import container traffic through the Far East ports grew by 41%, exports fell by 15%, and coastal (cabotage) traffic slipped by 3%. Every month about 125,000 import containers pass through the Far East terminals versus 40,000 export and 30,000 cabotage ones. The ratio is roughly four to one — and it is not just statistics but a structural skew that drives the entire market.

Terminals are drowning in inbound cargo, and Rossiyskaya Gazeta calls it an imbalance that puts extra strain on port terminals and rail infrastructure (TKS.RU, 17.11.2026).

The unevenness of cargo flows complicates container and rolling-stock turnover, increasing the risk of cargo piling up at ports.

The bottleneck is not where you think

The main pain of the Far East route is not sea discharge but hauling the container inland by rail. Ports currently ship out more than 4,000 containers a day against a target of 6,000. The number of containers waiting for dispatch has, according to market participants, already dropped by 24% — but the shortage of flatcars and queues for loading persist.

For the cargo owner the result is a stretched lead time: delivery of a container from China via Vladivostok to Moscow has grown from about 45 to 60 days. The sea leg is short — two or three days — but then the cargo hits the railway.

The market responds with unconventional solutions: containers are moved in open-top gondola cars, trains run on agreed schedules, and loading drops wooden securing — saving about 2,400 rubles per car and speeding up handling (TKS.RU, 17.11.2026).

Importantly, these are not amateur schemes but legitimized practices. RZD has been hauling containers in gondola cars since 2022: to stimulate evacuation it introduced a 20.7% discount — effective until the end of 2025 and later extended to the Zabaikalsk border crossing (Trans.ru). The scheme itself is enshrined in the Rules for the carriage of goods in containers (Ministry of Transport Order No. 405): the amendments introduced by Order No. 3 of 09.01.2025, effective from 22 February 2025, permit changing the rolling stock en route. Securing is governed by the technical conditions for stowage and securing of cargo: standard twistlock securing needs no timber, while non-standard schemes are formalized as local technical conditions agreed with the carrier.

A separate bet is on dry ports: the Artyom transport and logistics centre in Primorye alone is designed to handle 542,000 TEU per year (TKS.RU, 17.11.2026).

Anatomy of a rate: how $1010 became $2950

The most painful question is price. According to ATU Forwarding, in August 2026 freight for a 40-foot container from Shanghai to Vladivostok reached $2950 — 192% higher than a year earlier (against $1010 in August 2025). And this was no one-off spike: back in April the same $2950 rate was 146% above the year-ago level — against $1200 (Rambler/Finance).

The rise was distributed very unevenly across ports. Novorossiysk in August cost $8500 against $4000 a year earlier (+113%). St. Petersburg turned out to be almost the only major destination where freight got cheaper: $4400 against $4950 (−11%). Each port lives by its own economics — price is simultaneously driven by congestion, vessel availability, trade flows and geopolitical risks.

Shanghai → Vladivostok, 40ft: $2950 in August 2026 against $1010 a year earlier — +192%. For comparison: in April the same $2950 was against $1200 (+146%).

The spread between lines on the same leg reaches 40–140%: on the Qingdao–Vladivostok route two carriers quoted $1900 and $2358 for a 20-foot container, while a third quoted $4550 for a 40-foot one. The difference lies in the carriers' own container fleet, loading terms and slot availability on a given week (MEO, 30.09.2026). Kommersant noted back in August that container delivery from China to Moscow had risen to a 3.5-year record — the highest since 2023. The rise in the multimodal cost via the Far East ports was driven by a 9% monthly increase in sea freight and a 6% increase in the rail leg (Kommersant, 19.08.2026).

Why it happened

Three forces converged at one point. First, geopolitics: the escalation in the Azov-Black Sea basin squeezed alternative routes. FESCO, after the incident with the container ship Yanina in August, effectively wound down its Black Sea route and rebuilt logistics east and west, bypassing the Black Sea (Monocle, 24.08.2026). Part of the cargo that used to go via the south flooded into the already overloaded Far East ports.

Second, seasonality: November and December traditionally bring a demand surge as importers aim to get goods into Russian warehouses before the New Year. Third, the infrastructure bottleneck we have already mentioned: the short sea leg runs into an overloaded railway.

Who unloads Primorye — and how

While the railway scales up evacuation, the market is looking for detours. Among the projects is the Eastern export corridor through the Khasan–Tumangan crossing and the North Korean port of Rajin: initial transshipment of up to 200,000 tonnes per month, rising to 500,000 under stable load. An agreement with the port administration has been signed, but a full launch still requires technical, tariff and legal groundwork (TKS.RU, 17.11.2026).

In parallel, there is a push for partner diversification — starting with the Northern Sea Route. NewNew Shipping Line opened its China ↔ Arkhangelsk container service via the NSR back in 2023, and in March 2026 signed a memorandum of cooperation with the Arkhangelsk Region government (InfraNews, PortNews). India and Iran are also in the mix.

Vladivostok, meanwhile, is building export legs where there is backhaul demand. On the Vladivostok–Ho Chi Minh route, the export rate for a 40-foot SOC container holds at $1463 versus $2326 on imports, and for a 20-foot SOC it is $894 on export versus $1604 on import (October). If the container is carrier-owned (COC), the export rate matches SOC while the import rate is higher: $2955 for a 40-foot (+27%) and $1760 for a 20-foot (+9%). The average rate is calculated from seven lines — CK Line, FESCO, Hua Xin Lines, MSC, Panda Express Line, Sinokor and SITC (SeaNews, 08.10.2026).

What it means for trade participants

The Far East route at the end of 2026 is an expensive but predictably expensive corridor. Three practical takeaways to build into your work now.

First, budget for the rate increase. Wellgo, for example, expects delivery of a 40-foot container (40'HC) via the Far East to rise by 50–80 thousand rubles by December — a forecast based on the 2024–2025 seasonal dynamics, when the September-to-December tariff difference reached exactly these figures (RZD-Partner, 09.11.2026). This is a guide, not an announced tariff — but it is worth reserving for.

Second, don't rely on a single line. The rate spread between carriers reaches 40–140%, and slot availability changes weekly. Third, separate the sea and land legs in the contract — the risks and liability for rail-evacuation delays must be spelled out separately.

Write into your supply contracts — or, if you take cargo EXW/FCA from the supplier, into your freight-forwarding instructions — the use of cargo unsecuring in containers in line with the technical conditions for stowage and securing of cargo, to avoid delays and costs while containers wait to be re-secured. Don't take risks with goods subject to non-tariff regulation either: obtain the required declarations or TR/CU certificates in advance — before shipment from the supplier, or at the latest before arrival at the port.

And above all: if a shipment is time-critical, don't postpone dispatch to December. The cargo surge will coincide with the flatcar shortage — and unpredictability of lead times will add to the price. For urgent shipments it makes sense to consider road delivery or express trains from China (RZD-Partner, 09.11.2026).
