The Ice Corridor

The Northern Sea Route in 2026 has crossed 40 million tonnes for the first time in history, and Arctic transit is turning from an experiment into a full-fledged business. We look at why the "ice corridor" is no longer exotic — and what it means for a cargo owner moving freight between Europe and Asia.

ANALYTICS · NOVEMBER 25, 2026 · Vadim Chirkov

37 million — and it turned out not to be the ceiling

Not long ago the main question about the Northern Sea Route was: "when will it reach the declared volumes?" In 2025 the answer sounded restrained — about 37 million tonnes of cargo passed along the NSR, slightly below the record year of 2024 (Rossiyskaya Gazeta, 19.01.2026). Against the earlier plans (80 million tonnes by 2024) this looked like stagnation. But the real shift was already brewing inside these numbers — transit.

≈37 million tonnes — NSR cargo volume in 2025, the second-highest in the route's history

Transit traffic — what turns the NSR from domestic "Arctic cabotage" into an international corridor — reached a record 3.2 million tonnes in 2025 (+3.8% year-on-year) (PortNews, 18.11.2025). In 2026 the forecast was rewritten twice as boldly: transit may exceed 7 million tonnes (TKS.RU, 27.10.2026).

2026: the 40-million mark is passed

The historic mark was announced by Rosatom's head Alexey Likhachev: in November 2026 NSR cargo volumes exceeded 40 million tonnes for the first time (RIA Novosti, 18.11.2026). For comparison, back in 2016 the route carried just over 7 million tonnes — a manifold growth over a decade (Kommersant, 18.11.2026).

40 million tonnes — NSR cargo volume in 2026, an all-time record

The pace confirms the trend: in the first five months of 2026 the NSR carried 14.5 million tonnes — 13% more than a year earlier. The growth is no longer one-off but systemic: behind it is not sporadic shipment but the accumulated cargo base of Arctic projects.

Transit: China has got a taste for the Arctic

A symbolic moment came in autumn 2025: China delivered its goods to Europe in transit via the NSR for the first time (RIA Novosti, 19.10.2025). The first transit container ship carried 25,000 tonnes of cargo from the Chinese port of Ningbo to the British Felixstowe in just 20 days (23 September to 13 October). This places the Arctic route alongside the traditional southern corridors — not as a concept but as a working alternative to the Suez Canal.

Rosatom then issued China's Sealegend Shipping a permit for passage and agreed to organise a year-round container line between the two countries' ports (Interfax). "Rosatom has already issued permits for the passage of seven vessels in the NSR waters," Likhachev said. In parallel, the Chinese NewNew Shipping Line is already operating on the NSR — its voyages reach Arkhangelsk.

The NSR shortens the distance between Northern Europe and East Asia by 7,000 to 10,000 kilometres compared with the route via Suez.

The economics of the route look convincing for a cargo owner: the path from Korea to England via the Suez Canal is over 23,000 km, while through the Arctic it is radically shorter (InvestFunds). The gain is not only in kilometres but in weeks of delivery.

International interest: from Korea to India

A queue of Asian economies is lining up behind the NSR. South Korea sent its first-ever trial container voyage along the NSR in August 2026: the ship PanStar Acro (~2,800 TEU) left Busan on 22 August, passed through the British Felixstowe, the Dutch Rotterdam and the Polish Gdansk, then returned to Korea by the same route, completing a round trip (Rossiyskaya Gazeta, 22.08.2026) (TKS.RU, 10.09.2026). Seoul ties the route to a long-term strategy of turning Busan into a key logistics hub of Northeast Asia.

China has already moved from trial voyages to regular container shipments to Europe via the NSR. India is holding intergovernmental talks and has agreed with Moscow to grow NSR cargo flow to 5 million tonnes by 2030 — although so far, according to the Russian ambassador, the interest is "still potential" (RIA Novosti, 04.09.2026). Japan and other Asia-Pacific countries are also voicing interest in using the route (TKS.RU, 01.04.2026).

PanStar Acro — the first South Korean container ship to complete an NSR round trip (August–September 2026)

Icebreaker fleet: the foundation that holds it all up

No transit works without icebreakers. The core of the new generation is the universal nuclear icebreakers of Project 22220 — Arktika, Sibir, Ural, Yakutia. They can change draught and work both at sea and in river mouths — this is what makes year-round escort technically possible.

8 nuclear icebreakers in service today; 11 by 2030, 18 by 2035

The lead ship, the icebreaker Arktika, has covered 126,166 miles and escorted 677 vessels in five years of service (Rosatom Bulletin, 28.10.2025). Today 8 nuclear icebreakers are in service and 4 more are under construction (AARI). The fleet is now being built up fast: by 2030 the number of nuclear icebreakers serving the NSR is planned to reach 11, including 7–8 Project 22220 ships, and by 2035 the fleet will grow to 18, including the new Project 10510 "Leader" nuclear icebreakers (Rossiyskaya Gazeta, 19.01.2026).

The super-powerful "Leader" (Project 10510, LK-120Ya) is not just "another icebreaker" but a change of class. With 120 MW on the shafts it is twice as powerful as the current 22220 (60 MW), and in size — 209.2 m long and 47.7 m wide — it exceeds even an average aircraft carrier: full displacement reaches 71,380 tonnes (Korabel.ru). The wide hull allows it to break a channel about 50 metres wide — enough for large gas carriers and tankers of over 100,000 tonnes deadweight.

Full Leader specifications: speed — up to 24 knots, range — unlimited, endurance in provisions — 8 months, icebreaking capability — 4.3 m at 2 knots and 2.0 m at 15 knots (Korabel.ru). For comparison, Project 22220 icebreakers pass ice up to 3 metres thick (Iceberg Central Design Bureau).

The lead ship of the series, Rossiya, is being built at the Zvezda shipyard in Primorye. Under the current schedule, launching is set for March 2028 and delivery to the fleet for December 2029. The arrival of the Leaders is tied to opening year-round navigation in the eastern sector of the NSR — the hardest in terms of ice conditions.

It is the Leader that must open year-round navigation in the eastern sector of the NSR — the hardest in terms of ice. For now, navigation in the eastern part remains seasonal, and in 2026 the first LNG-laden vessel set out in late May — almost a month earlier than in 2025 (RBC, 29.05.2026).

Cargo base: from Yamal to Vostok Oil

Three Arctic mega-projects form the NSR's cargo flow. The first is the operating Yamal LNG at Sabetta, which set the standard for year-round LNG shipment. The second is Arctic LNG 2, which broke through the export blockade: the first line started in December 2023, the second in May 2025, and by autumn 2025 exports from the project exceeded 1.2 million tonnes (Monocle) (RBC).

The third and largest is the Vostok Oil oil project with reserves of 51 billion barrels. In September 2026 the project's first oil travelled from the field to the new Bukhta Sever terminal on the Taymyr peninsula and was loaded onto the Arc 6 ice-class tanker Valentin Pikul (MK, 22.09.2026) (GoArctic). It is Vostok Oil that should provide the main growth of the NSR's cargo base towards 2030.

NSR ports: the west holds, the east catches up

The route's infrastructure splits into three tiers. The western flank holds the most powerful and busiest ports. Murmansk — the only ice-free port of the Russian Arctic and the home base of the nuclear icebreaker fleet — receives vessels with draught up to 15.5 m and deadweight up to 150,000 tonnes, with cargo turnover exceeding 31 million tonnes in 2025 (Murmansk port review). Sabetta is the anchor export terminal of Yamal LNG with capacity of 19.5 million t/year and berth depths of 15.2 m (Sabetta port). Dudinka on the Yenisey is the gateway to the Norilsk industrial area: 8 sea berths, 112 portal cranes, capacity of 1.885 million t/year (Dudinka port).

The eastern flank is still markedly weaker — and that is the main imbalance. Pevek, the northernmost port of Russia, receives vessels up to 177 m long with berth depths of 7.8–10 m; its capacity is only 330,000 t/year (Pevek port). Dikson (200,000 t/year) is set to grow under Vostok Oil: an 10-million-tonne coal terminal "Chaika" and a 25-million-tonne oil terminal are planned here (Dikson port). In September 2026 Tiksi began building the deep-water terminal "Nayba" for containers from China (Izvestia, 16.09.2026). It is the eastern sector that the Leader icebreaker is meant to "open".

In the future the NSR's growth points are the port of Dikson, where coal and oil terminals are planned, and Tiksi, where construction of a container terminal has already begun.

Targets: 70 million instead of the previous 150

The original NSR development plans set 150 million tonnes by 2030; reality forced a recalculation. In the updated version the target is more modest but more achievable — at least 70 million tonnes by 2030 (TKS.RU, 22.10.2026).

70 million tonnes by 2030 — the revised NSR target (previous plan: 150 million tonnes)

For a professional this is not a "retreat" but a calibration to the real cargo base: 37 million in 2025 against 70 million by 2030 is almost a doubling, tied to Vostok Oil and Arctic LNG 2 reaching full capacity. An indirect upper reference: materials of the presidential envoy in the Far Eastern Federal District mention 109.7 million tonnes by 2030 and 151 million by 2035, leaving room for an optimistic scenario.

How the NSR shapes the freight market

By the end of 2026 the NSR is no longer a "project for the future" but a route with a growing schedule and clear economics. Practical takeaways:

— Shorter leg. The Europe–Asia route through the Arctic is 7–10 thousand km shorter than Suez; the saving is weeks of delivery. — A year-round service is taking shape. The Rosatom deal with Sealegend and Chinese lines turns seasonal navigation into a regular container service. — Transit grows faster than cabotage. 3.2 million tonnes in 2025 → 7 million in 2026; transit is the fastest-growing component. — Ice class is mandatory. Working on the NSR requires ice-class vessels and icebreaker escort — this filters operators and keeps rates above the southern routes. — The eastern sector is still seasonal. Year-round navigation there is tied to icebreaker escort, which still affects the specifics of shipments in the region.

The key strategic conclusion: the NSR has stopped being a "just in case" alternative and is becoming the third corridor between Europe and Asia — alongside Suez and the Trans-Siberian. For cargo owners working with northern ports and time-sensitive freight, this already affects route choice and budgeting.

Figures are from open sources and current as of 25.11.2026. Rates, volumes and schedules on the NSR change quickly — for real transactions check conditions with the lines and the icebreaker operator.
