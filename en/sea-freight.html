International sea freight

Maritime transport is the backbone of international trade: about 90% of world cargo volume (by weight) moves by sea. It is the most cost-efficient way to deliver large consignments over long distances.

Vessel types

Container ships

They carry standard containers and are classified by capacity (in TEU — twenty-foot equivalent units):

ClassCapacityNotes
Feederup to ~3,000 TEUshort-haul, feeder services to major hubs
Panamax~3,000–4,500 TEUfits the old locks of the Panama Canal
Post-Panamax~5,000–9,000 TEUtoo wide for the old locks
New Panamax~10,000–14,000 TEUthe new (expanded) locks of the Panama Canal
ULCV / ULCS18,000–24,000+ TEUultra-large, only on the Asia–Europe trade

Example: the largest ULCV-class container ship — MSC Irina

MSC Irina (2023) is one of the largest container ships in the world, the lead vessel in a series of 24,000+ TEU ships built for Mediterranean Shipping Company (MSC). It operates on the main Asia–Europe route.

Because of their dimensions (a 61 m beam) and draught, such vessels can only call at a limited number of deep-water hub ports with modern terminals and heavy-duty crane equipment. The 24,000+ TEU capacity is achieved by stacking containers up to 10–11 tiers in the hold and up to 12–13 tiers on deck.

How to read a container's stowage position on board

The position of each container on a container ship is defined by three coordinates — Bay / Row / Tier:

CoordinateMeaningNumbering rule
BayCross-section of the vessel, counted from bow to sternodd numbers (01, 03, 05…) — for 20-foot containers; even numbers (02, 04, 06…) — for 40-foot containers
RowTransverse row from the centreline towards the sideseven numbers (02, 04, 06…) — starboard; odd numbers (01, 03, 05…) — port
TierVertical level from the bottom of the hold upwardseven numbers (02, 04, 06…) — in the hold; 82, 84, 86… — on deck (numbering continues upward)

Example of a stowage position: the container 034282 means — Bay 03 (a 20-foot bay), Row 42 (starboard), Tier 82 (first tier on deck). This code is used in cargo plans, terminal documents and load/discharge planning.

Bulk carriers

They carry dry-bulk cargoes (ore, coal, grain, fertilisers) and are classified by deadweight (DWT):

ClassDeadweight (DWT)Use
Handysize15,000–35,000 tsmall ports, versatility
Supramax35,000–60,000 tmedium parcels, with deck cranes
Panamax60,000–80,000 tsized for the Panama Canal
Capesize100,000–400,000 tore/coal, routes around the Cape of Good Hope

Example: a Capesize bulk carrier — Valemax

Valemax is a series of the world's largest ore carriers with a deadweight of ~400,000 t, built for the Brazilian mining company Vale to carry iron ore from Brazil to China. The lead vessel is Vale Brasil (2011).

ParameterValue
Deadweight~400,000 t
Length~362 m
Beam~65 m
Draught~23 m
Useiron ore, Brazil → China route
Built2011–2016 (China, South Korea, Philippines)

Valemax vessels pass through neither the Panama nor the Suez Canal (the latter is too small for them when fully laden) and sail around the Cape of Good Hope. The huge size of a single voyage (up to 400,000 t of ore) gives the lowest cost per tonne, so such ships are only economically justified on stable, high-volume ore routes between the largest deep-water terminals.

Other vessel types

  • Tanker — liquid cargoes: crude oil and petroleum products, chemicals (chemical tanker), liquefied gas LNG/LPG (gas carrier).
  • Ro-Ro — wheeled cargo: cars, trailers, special equipment (driven on board under their own power).
  • General cargo / multipurpose vessel — break-bulk and general cargo in packaging, often with its own cranes.
  • Reefer vessel — perishable cargo under controlled temperature.
  • Heavy-lift vessel — oversized and extra-heavy equipment (project cargo).

Specialised containers

  • Reefer — with a built-in refrigeration unit, for temperature-controlled cargo (from −35 °C to +25 °C).
  • Open Top — opening top (tarpaulin), for cargo loaded from above by crane or over-height cargo.
  • Flat Rack — a platform without walls or roof, for heavy and oversized cargo (machinery, equipment).
  • Tank container — a tank within a container frame, for liquid cargoes.
  • Open Side — opening side walls, for cargo that cannot be loaded through the doors.
Practical tip: when choosing a container, check the weight limits — the payload depends on the type, as well as the carrier's and port's limits on the gross container weight. For heavy cargo (ore, metal), the volume is often not fully used — the weight is the limiting factor.
